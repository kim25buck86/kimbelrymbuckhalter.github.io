## About My Project

My DREAM Apprenticeship research project is titled "Expanding the Future Computing Workforce Through Computer Science Education Pathways in Mississippi."
This project examines how computer science education pathways can contribute to developing Mississippi's future computing workforce. Through a review of existing literature and available data, the study explores participation, access, and success in computer science education across the state. The research focuses on factors such as race, ethnicity, gender, geographic location, and educational opportunities that may influence student participation in computer science. The goal is to identify challenges, opportunities, and strategies that can strengthen computer science education pathways and support a more diverse and prepared computing workforce in Mississippi.

# Week 2 Blog: Gender Representation in Computer Science: Challenges and Opportunities

As I begin my DREAM research project, I wanted to first explore one of the most frequently discussed topics in computer science education: the underrepresentation of women in computing. Although computer science is one of the fastest-growing fields in the United States, women continue to be significantly underrepresented in both educational programs and technology careers.

My initial literature review revealed that women have historically played important roles in computing. Early computer programming relied heavily on women, and many women made significant contributions to the development of modern computing technologies. However, over time, cultural stereotypes and changing perceptions of the profession contributed to a decline in female participation in computer science (Nowak, 2024). Women who were once central to the field became increasingly underrepresented as computing became viewed as a male-dominated profession.

Several studies identified barriers that continue to affect women's participation in computer science today. Common challenges include gender stereotypes, bias, microaggressions, and a lack of representation in educational and professional settings. These factors can negatively impact confidence, sense of belonging, and persistence within the field (Kuzerbayeva, Sahin, & Sathya, 2024).

Another important theme that emerged from the literature was the importance of community and support networks. Research from Northwestern University found that women-in-computing organizations can help students develop confidence, build a sense of belonging, and strengthen their identity as computer science students. These groups provide supportive environments that encourage women to remain engaged in computing education and careers. The study also noted that women's representation among computer science degree recipients declined from more than 35% in the 1980s to approximately 21.9% in 2021.

Additional research highlighted how universities are working to improve female participation in computing and data science programs. Boston University reported that women make up only about 15–20% of data science professionals nationally, yet intentional recruitment and retention efforts have helped increase female enrollment in their computing programs. These findings suggest that institutions can play an important role in creating pathways that attract and support women in computing fields.

Finally, research examining computer systems conferences found that women represented only about 10% of researchers in that area of computer science. The authors concluded that increasing participation will require addressing deeper systemic issues rather than relying solely on diversity initiatives.

Overall, the literature suggests that increasing female participation in computer science requires more than recruitment efforts alone. Building supportive communities, addressing stereotypes, providing mentorship, and creating accessible educational pathways may help broaden participation in computing.

While the studies reviewed in this blog focus primarily on national trends, they provide an important foundation for understanding gender representation in computer science. As this research project continues, I will explore how these trends compare to opportunities and challenges within Mississippi. Understanding the experiences of women in computing at both the national and state levels may help identify strategies for expanding participation in graduate computer science education and strengthening Mississippi's future computing workforce.

# Week 3 Blog: Broadening the Lens – Representation in Computer Science

After exploring gender representation in computer science during Week 2, I began broadening my examination of underrepresentation in computing. One of the ideas that has become increasingly important to my research is that underrepresentation at the graduate level cannot necessarily be understood by looking only at graduate enrollment. Students arrive at the point of considering graduate school after years of educational experiences that may influence their preparation, confidence, opportunities, and knowledge of computing careers.

Access to computer science coursework, research experiences, internships, mentoring, professional networks, and information about graduate education can potentially influence whether students continue along a computing pathway. This means that understanding graduate computer science participation requires considering both who enters the pathway and what happens as students progress through it.

I also began thinking more specifically about representation by race and ethnicity. Examining multiple dimensions of representation is important because disparities within computing are not experienced in exactly the same way by every group. Understanding these differences can provide a more complete picture of participation in computer science education.

Graduate education is particularly important because master's and doctoral programs can provide pathways to advanced technical positions, research careers, leadership roles, and faculty positions. Therefore, disparities in graduate participation may have implications that extend beyond university enrollment and into the future computing workforce and academic research community.

As my project continues, I want to better understand how graduate computer science enrollment patterns compare across demographic groups and how those patterns connect to the larger issue of broadening participation in computing.

# Week 4 Blog: Understanding Barriers to Graduate Computer Science Education

This week, I focused on developing a better understanding of the factors that may contribute to underrepresentation in graduate computer science education. As my research continues to develop, I am learning that disparities in graduate participation are unlikely to result from one single barrier.

Several areas are emerging as important to examine through the literature, including access to academic preparation and research opportunities, recruitment and admissions practices, financial barriers, mentorship and professional networks, sense of belonging, departmental climate, and representation among graduate students and faculty.

## Academic Preparation and Research Opportunities

Students enter the graduate-school decision-making process with different educational experiences. Access to computer science coursework, programming experiences, internships, undergraduate research, and other forms of preparation may influence whether students feel prepared to pursue advanced study in computer science.

This is particularly relevant when considering students whose undergraduate degrees are not in computer science but who may still have an interest in transitioning into the field.

## Financial Barriers

Financial considerations may also affect graduate-school decisions. Tuition, fees, living expenses, and uncertainty about financial assistance can influence whether graduate education appears attainable.

This makes financial information and institutional support potentially important parts of efforts to broaden participation.

## Mentorship and Professional Networks

Mentorship is another factor I am interested in exploring. Faculty members, advisors, peers, and computing professionals can help students learn about graduate education, research opportunities, careers, and the graduate application process.

Students who have limited access to these networks may have fewer opportunities to receive this type of guidance.

## Belonging and Representation

Another area of interest is the role of belonging and representation. Students' experiences within computing environments may influence whether they see themselves continuing in the field.

Representation among students and faculty may therefore be connected not only to recruitment but also to students' experiences within computing programs.

Looking at these potential barriers together has helped me understand why increasing graduate computer science participation may require multiple approaches rather than a single recruitment strategy.

## Reflection

One of my biggest challenges during this stage of the project has been narrowing a very broad issue into research questions that can realistically be addressed.

There are many questions I would like to investigate about students' experiences, confidence, belonging, mentoring relationships, and decisions about graduate education. However, I am learning that researchers must distinguish between questions they would like to answer and questions that can be answered using the data available to them.

This has been an important lesson for me as I continue developing as a researcher.

My guiding question moving forward is:

**Which barriers to graduate computer science participation can potentially be addressed through programs designed to create alternative pathways into master's-level computer science education?**

# Week 6 Blog: Understanding the Design of Graduate CS Bridge Programs

During Week 6 of my DREAM research experience, I continued examining bridge programs designed to create alternative pathways into graduate computer science education. My focus this week shifted from understanding why bridge programs exist to examining how these programs are designed.

One program that has been particularly useful to my research is Northeastern University's Align MS in Computer Science program. Brodley et al. (2020) describe Align as a pathway for postsecondary graduates who did not major in computer science. Rather than requiring students to enter with a traditional undergraduate CS background, the program includes a two-semester bridge curriculum that prepares students to transition into master's-level computer science coursework.

This model helped me think differently about access to graduate computer science. Traditional pathways may assume that students interested in an MS in Computer Science have already completed substantial undergraduate CS coursework. A bridge program creates another possibility by asking how students with different academic backgrounds can be intentionally prepared for graduate-level study.

I also identified recent research by Kidder et al. (2026) examining the structure and scope of bridge-to-computing master's programs. This research is especially relevant to my project because it places bridge programs within a broader area of graduate computing education rather than treating them as isolated institutional initiatives.

## From Program Design to Program Evaluation

One of my biggest realizations this week was that I cannot meaningfully evaluate a program until I clearly understand what the program was designed to accomplish.

Before examining outcomes, I need to understand several characteristics of the program in my DREAM study:

* Why was the program created?
* Who is it designed to serve?
* What are its goals?
* What bridge courses are included?
* How are participants recruited and selected?
* What support is provided?
* What changed during its first two years?
* What outcomes were expected?

These questions are important because the goals of a program should determine how its success is evaluated.

For example, if a bridge program is intended to provide pathways for students without traditional undergraduate CS preparation, then participants' academic backgrounds, completion of bridge coursework, admission, and enrollment may be important outcomes to examine.

## Reflection

This week helped me understand the difference between **describing an intervention and evaluating an intervention**.

It would be easy to look at enrollment numbers or participant demographics and immediately begin drawing conclusions. However, those numbers only become meaningful when they are interpreted in relation to the program's goals and design.

I am also learning to separate what the literature tells me about bridge programs generally from what I know about the specific program being evaluated in my research. Programs may share a common purpose while differing in their structure, participants, implementation, and measures of success.

This is an important distinction that I will need to maintain throughout the project.

## Next Steps

My next step is to work with my research advisor to develop a detailed understanding of the specific bridge program being evaluated. I will focus on its origins, goals, target population, bridge courses, participant-selection process, implementation, and available data.

This information will help me transition from the literature-review stage into the program-evaluation stage of my DREAM research.
